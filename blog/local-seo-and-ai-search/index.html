Local SEO in the Age of AI Search: A Practical Guide

Published September 21, 2026 by Wesley Hopkins

"Best divorce lawyer in Huntsville." "HVAC company near me that's open on Sunday." "Emergency plumber, Springfield." Local questions like these are some of the most valuable searches there are, because the person asking is often ready to call. They're also increasingly answered by an AI summary or an assistant before anyone clicks a link.

For local businesses, the inputs to those answers look different from a national content site's. Your website matters, but it's frequently not the most influential source. This post covers the parts of local visibility that carry the most weight.

Google says local data matters

In its guide to generative AI search, Google points specifically to Business Profiles for local visibility and Merchant Center for ecommerce, alongside its advice on content and technical foundations. That's a strong hint about where local AI answers get their facts: structured business data Google already has and trusts.

Other assistants have their own sources, and none of them publish the full list. It's reasonable to expect they draw on similar raw material (business listings, reviews, and pages that describe what you do and where), so getting those right covers most fronts.

1. Own and complete your Google Business Profile

Your profile is the single most important local asset. Claim it, verify it, and fill it out completely and truthfully:

  • Primary category: the most specific one that describes your core business. This matters more than most fields.
  • Secondary categories: add those that truly apply, not every category you can think of.
  • Services and products: list what you offer, with clear names and short descriptions.
  • Hours, including holidays. Keep them current. Wrong hours are a top source of bad answers.
  • Business description: plain and factual. Same wording as elsewhere.
  • Service area, if you travel to customers. Define it honestly.
  • Photos: real ones of your work, team, and location, added regularly.
  • Website and contact details that match your site exactly.

Set a monthly reminder to review it. Profiles go stale, and anyone can suggest edits to them.

2. Earn genuine reviews, steadily

Reviews are both a ranking input for local results and a source of descriptive text that AI systems read. A review that says "fixed our furnace on a Sunday night in January" tells a system what you do, when, and how well.

  • Ask every happy customer, right after the job while it's fresh. Make it easy with a direct link to your review form.
  • Respond to reviews, good and bad, professionally and specifically. A thoughtful reply to a critical review often helps more than a stack of five stars.
  • Aim for volume and recency. A steady trickle looks natural and stays current; a burst of fifty in a week looks suspicious.
  • Don't gate or buy reviews. Asking only satisfied customers, offering payment for reviews, and writing your own violate platform rules, and the FTC finalized a rule in 2024 banning fake reviews. The reputational cost of getting caught is larger than any upside. We dug into the wider problem in Inside the Manufactured Web.

If you work in a regulated field like law, medicine, or finance, check your professional rules on testimonials and how you may solicit or display them. They vary by jurisdiction and can be stricter than platform policies.

3. Make your listings agree with each other

Your name, address, and phone number should be identical everywhere you appear. Inconsistencies (an old address, a tracking number on one listing, "Street" versus "St.") force systems to guess whether these are the same business. Audit these places first:

  • Google Business Profile
  • Bing Places and Apple Business Connect, which feed Bing (and Microsoft's Copilot) and Apple's products
  • Major general directories such as Yelp and the Better Business Bureau
  • Your industry's directories, like Avvo or Justia for lawyers, Healthgrades for medical practices, or Angi for home services
  • Your social profiles

AI answers to local questions often include or draw on directory and review sites, so it's worth checking which ones appear when you ask for the best provider in your category and city. Those are the places to be strong. This is the local version of the consistency work described in Entity SEO.

4. Build service and location pages that are genuinely useful

Your website should state, clearly and specifically, what you do and where. For each major service, one dedicated page answers the questions a customer actually has: what's included, typical cost ranges, how long it takes, how to prepare, and who it's for.

Location pages are useful when they contain real local substance: the neighborhoods you serve, projects you've done there, local regulations or conditions that affect the work, and staff who work in that area. Avoid the old trick of cloning one page fifty times with only the city name swapped. It's thin content, and it's exactly the commodity material AI systems don't need to cite.

A good local page might include:

  • A direct, one-paragraph description of the service and the area at the top
  • Licenses, insurance, and certifications, with numbers where appropriate
  • Real photos and a specific example project
  • Answers to the local questions you actually get (permits, seasonal issues, pricing norms)
  • Clear contact options and hours

Write these pages using the principles in How to Write Content AI Search Engines Cite, and link them into a cluster: a service hub page that leads to each specific service and location.

5. Add local structured data

Mark up your homepage or contact page with LocalBusiness (or a more specific subtype such as Attorney, Plumber, or HVACBusiness), including name, address, phone, hours, and service area, and match it to your visible page and your profile. The details, with a working example, are in Schema Markup for AI Search.

6. Check what AI actually says about you

Try the questions your customers would ask, in the assistants they'd use:

  • "Best [service] in [city]"
  • "[Service] near [neighborhood] open now"
  • "Is [your business] reputable?"
  • "How much does [service] cost in [city]?"

Look at who's named, which sources are cited, and whether the facts about you are right. Wrong information usually comes from a stale listing or a page that isn't clear. Fix it at the source. Because answers vary between runs, do it repeatedly and record the results; the tracking guide shows how.

A local visibility checklist

  1. Google Business Profile claimed, verified, complete, and reviewed monthly
  2. Bing Places and Apple Business Connect listings set up
  3. Identical name, address, and phone across your site and all listings
  4. A consistent process for asking every satisfied customer for a review
  5. Responses to all reviews, especially critical ones
  6. A dedicated, useful page per core service, and real location pages where warranted
  7. LocalBusiness schema that matches your visible details
  8. Presence in the top directories for your industry
  9. A monthly check of local prompts in the major assistants
